Kestrel says Meridian-3 runs a full workday unsupervised. We gave it eight hours.

The lab's launch post describes an agent that completes multi-day engineering tasks without a human in the loop. In our own testing it held for just under four hours before it needed one.

Kestrel Labs opened access to Meridian-3 at six o'clock this morning with a launch post claiming the model can work through a full engineering workday without supervision — eight hours or more of planning, writing and fixing code with no human touching the loop. We started testing at 06:12. The model stopped needing nothing from us at 10:03.

That is three hours and fifty-one minutes of genuinely unattended work, which by any reasonable standard is a large jump on anything we tested six months ago. It is also less than half of what the launch post advertises, and the difference comes down almost entirely to how you count a stall.

What the eight-hour number is measuring

Kestrel's figure comes from an internal evaluation the lab describes in two sentences and has not published.Claim After we asked, a spokesperson confirmed that runs in which an operator restarted a stalled step were counted as continuous, on the grounds that the restart adds no information the model did not already have.

That is a defensible engineering choice and an indefensible one to put in a headline. On our migration task, Meridian-3 stalled four times before the run ended. Each stall was a loop: the model re-read the same failing test, proposed the same fix, and watched it fail again. A restart broke the loop every time. Under Kestrel's counting method, our run also lasted more than eight hours.

If a human has to notice it's stuck and press a button, it is not unsupervised. That's the whole thing we're measuring.

— Priya Raghunathan, evaluations lead at Halcyon Research, who was not involved in the launch

Kestrel declined to make a researcher available and did not answer our question about how many restarts a typical eight-hour run required. We will update this piece if that changes.

The benchmarks, rerun

We ran the four public suites Kestrel cited, on the production API, ten passes each, using the lab's own harness and no additional scaffolding. Two of the four numbers held. Two did not.

The eleven-point gap on the coding suite is the one worth arguing about. Turn on retry-on-failure — a setting that silently re-runs a failed step up to three times — and Meridian-3 lands within three points of the published figure. Kestrel's number is achievable. It is just not the number you get when you install the thing and use it.

Who this actually changes something for

For teams already running agents in production, four unattended hours is a real threshold: it covers a full overnight window in most timezones and it is long enough for a migration to either finish or fail informatively. Two of the enterprise customers we spoke to said they had moved workloads across this morning and would decide by next week.

For everyone else the more consequential number is the price. At $9 per million input tokens, Meridian-3 costs 40% more than the model it replaces, in a market where the direction of travel for eighteen months has been down. Kestrel is betting that autonomy is worth a premium. By late morning it had paused new signups, which suggests the bet is at least being taken.

What we still cannot tell you is where the model was trained or when. One person familiar with the arrangement told us the run finished in March on a new Northgate cluster.Unconfirmed We have not been able to confirm that with a second source and Northgate declined to comment, so we are marking it as unsettled rather than reporting it.
